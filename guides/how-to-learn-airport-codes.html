How to learn airport codes fast

A practical method for memorising airport codes: start with patterns, group by region, learn the look-alikes, and practise a little every day.

By Amateurgames1 · Published 16 November 2026

There are thousands of airport codes, but you don't need to know them all. Most frequent flyers and quiz fans get a long way with the busiest few hundred. Here's a method that works, whether you're preparing for a quiz, a job in travel, or just want to read a departures board without squinting.

1. Start with the codes that spell the name

A big share of codes are simply the first letters of the city or airport: DXB Dubai, SIN Singapore, AMS Amsterdam, MAD Madrid, BCN Barcelona. Learn these first. They build confidence and give you a base to compare the odd ones against.

2. Learn the patterns

Once the easy codes are in, learn the rules that explain whole groups at once:

  • Canadian codes start with Y: YYZ, YVR, YUL. See why.
  • US codes avoid N, K and W at the start, so Newark is EWR. See why.
  • Many codes keep an old name: BOM Bombay, PEK Peking, ORD Orchard Field. See the list.
  • Some follow the local name or the town next door: MUC München, FCO Fiumicino. See more.

3. Group by region

Your brain remembers things in groups better than in a long list. Learn London's six airports together, then Paris, then the big US hubs, then the Gulf. When you practise, stick to one region at a time until it's solid. Our Quick Play rounds are split by region for exactly this reason.

4. Learn the look-alikes on purpose

Most mistakes come from similar codes, not unknown ones. LHR and LGW, IAD and IAH, SJO and SJU, BNA and BNE. When you learn a code, look up the codes that are one letter away from it. Every one of our airport pages has a "Don't mix it up with" list for this.

5. Attach a story

A code with a story is far easier to remember than three random letters. "LAX has an X because Los Angeles used to be LA" sticks. "CDG is Charles de Gaulle's initials" sticks. Even a silly link works: YUL as the "Yule" airport.

6. Little and often

Memory research is clear that short, spaced practice beats long cramming sessions. Five minutes a day for a month will do more than an afternoon once. That's the idea behind our Daily Departure: five codes a day, a streak to keep you coming back, and a mix of easy and tricky codes.

7. Test yourself in both directions

Knowing that CPT is Cape Town isn't the same as knowing Cape Town's code. Practise both ways: code to airport, and airport to code. Quick Play does both.
