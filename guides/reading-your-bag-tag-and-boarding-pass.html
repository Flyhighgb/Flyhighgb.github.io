How to read your bag tag and boarding pass

The big three letters, the ten-digit number, the barcode and the odd abbreviations. What your bag tag and boarding pass are telling you.

By Amateurgames1 · Published 16 November 2026

Airport codes matter most at the moment you hand over your bag. The tag the agent prints decides where it goes, and a quick check can save you a lost suitcase. Here's what's on it, and on your boarding pass.

The bag tag

The biggest thing on a bag tag is the three-letter code of your final destination, printed large so baggage handlers can read it at a glance. If you're connecting, the tag also lists each stop, in order, often with the flight number for each leg.

Check it before the bag disappears. If you're flying to Portland, Oregon, the tag should say PDX, not PWM (Portland, Maine). If you're going to Sydney, Australia, it should say SYD. Mix-ups between similar codes do happen, and the agent's screen is the last chance to catch one.

The ten-digit number

Each tag carries a ten-digit number, sometimes called the licence plate. The first digit is often 0, and the next three usually identify the airline that issued the tag, using its numeric airline code. British Airways is 125, for example. The last six digits identify your bag. You'll get a matching stub, often stuck to your boarding pass. Keep it: if your bag goes missing, that number is how the airline finds it.

The barcode

The barcode holds the same ten-digit number. Automated sorting systems scan it and look up where the bag should go, which is why the big printed code is mainly for people and the barcode does most of the work.

Your boarding pass

A boarding pass packs a lot into a small space:

  • From and to: the airport codes for this flight, such as LHR to JFK.
  • Flight number: the two-character airline code plus a number, such as BA117. See airline codes vs airport codes.
  • Booking reference: a six-character code, also called a PNR, used to find your booking.
  • Seat and boarding group or zone.
  • Gate and boarding time, though gates can change, so check the screens.
  • Sequence number: the order in which passengers checked in.
  • Barcode: holds your name, flight, seat and booking details, so it can be scanned at security and the gate.

Abbreviations you might see

  • SSSS on a US boarding pass means you've been selected for extra security screening.
  • INF means you're travelling with an infant on your lap.
  • Terminal codes, such as T5 at Heathrow, tell you where to go before you even reach the airport.

Why it helps to know the codes

Departures boards, bag tags, connecting-flight signs and booking sites all use the same three letters. Knowing the codes for the airports you use, and the ones they're easily confused with, makes travel noticeably less stressful. Each of our airport pages lists the codes most often mixed up with it.
