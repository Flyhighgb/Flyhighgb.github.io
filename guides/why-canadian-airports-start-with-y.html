Why do Canadian airports start with Y?

Toronto is YYZ, Vancouver is YVR, Montréal is YUL. Here's why nearly every big Canadian airport begins with a Y.

By Amateurgames1 · Published 5 November 2026 · Updated 16 November 2026

Look at a Canadian boarding pass and you'll almost always see a Y. Toronto Pearson is YYZ, Vancouver is YVR, Montréal is YUL, Calgary is YYC and Ottawa is YOW. Edmonton (YEG), Winnipeg (YWG), Halifax (YHZ), Québec City (YQB) and Victoria (YYJ) follow the same rule. No other country uses a single letter so consistently, which makes Canada one of the easiest places to spot on a departures board and one of the hardest to tell apart.

Where the Y came from

The usual explanation goes back to the early days of radio. Canada gave its weather and radio stations two-letter identifiers. When three-letter airport codes became the standard, Canada added a Y in front of the old two letters, and the habit stuck. One common account is that the Y marked a weather station that shared its site with an airport. Historians differ on the details, but the result is clear: Y now means Canada almost everywhere you look.

The same idea carries over to the four-letter codes pilots use. Those are set by ICAO, and Canada's all begin with C, so Toronto Pearson is CYYZ and Vancouver is CYVR. You can read more about the two systems in our guide to IATA and ICAO codes.

Why the letters after the Y look random

Because the last two letters came from those old station identifiers, many don't match the city's name at all. That's why Toronto is YYZ and not something like YTO. YTO does exist, but as a city code that covers all of Toronto's airports, including the small Billy Bishop airport on the Toronto Islands, which is YTZ. Montréal has a city code too, YMQ.

Some codes do line up with the name. In YVR you can see Vancouver's V and R, in YOW the W of Ottawa, and in YQB the Q and B of Québec. Others, like YYC for Calgary and YEG for Edmonton, have no obvious link, so they simply have to be learned.

A code with a fan club

YYZ might be the most famous airport code in music. The Canadian rock band Rush named an instrumental track "YYZ" on their 1981 album Moving Pictures, and its opening rhythm spells the letters out in Morse code, the way the airport's radio beacon did.

Not every Y is Canadian

Y isn't reserved for Canada worldwide. Australia's four-letter ICAO codes start with Y as well (Sydney is YSSY), but its three-letter codes, the ones on your ticket, don't: Sydney is SYD and Melbourne is MEL. If a three-letter code starts with Y, it is almost certainly in Canada.

How to remember them

  • Ignore the Y and look at the last two letters: YVR, Vancouver; YOW, Ottawa; YQB, Québec.
  • Group them by region. In the west: YVR, YYC, YEG and YYJ. In the middle: YWG. In the east: YYZ, YOW, YUL, YQB and YHZ.
  • Toronto, Calgary and Edmonton are the odd ones out, so learn YYZ, YYC and YEG by heart. Two of them have a double Y, which helps.
  • Montréal's YUL is easiest to remember as the "Yule" airport. Corny, but it works.
