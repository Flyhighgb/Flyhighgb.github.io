Who decides airport codes, and how?

There are 17,576 possible three-letter codes. Here's who hands them out, what they look for, and why a code almost never changes.

By Amateurgames1 · Published 16 November 2026

The three letters on your bag tag aren't chosen by the airport alone. They're assigned by IATA, the International Air Transport Association, the trade body for the world's airlines. IATA publishes the list of codes and makes sure no two locations share one.

How many codes are there?

With 26 letters in each of three places, there are 26 × 26 × 26 = 17,576 possible codes. That sounds like plenty, but thousands of airports, heliports, railway stations and bus terminals need one. Some codes are also kept back or avoided, and many good combinations were taken early. So the supply of sensible codes ran low a long time ago.

How a code is chosen

An airport, or the country's aviation authority, applies to IATA for a code. The ideal code spells out something obvious: the city, the airport's name or a well-known nickname. LHR for London Heathrow and DXB for Dubai are the easy kind. If that combination is already taken, the applicant tries another set of letters from the name, which is how you get codes like EWR for Newark.

In the United States, the Federal Aviation Administration also gives airports its own identifiers, and for airports with airline service these usually match the IATA code. The US avoids certain first letters altogether, which you can read about in why US airport codes avoid N, K and W.

Why codes almost never change

An airport code is built into everything: airline reservation systems, timetables, fare tables, baggage sorting machines, printed signs and decades of records. Changing one is expensive and risky, because a bag tagged with the old code might end up somewhere else. So codes outlive airport names, city names and sometimes the airport itself. That's why Chicago O'Hare is still ORD, from Orchard Field, and Mumbai is still BOM, from Bombay.

The main exception is when a city opens a new airport and moves its main code across, as Istanbul did with IST in 2019. We cover those cases in airports that moved their code.

Codes for places that aren't airports

Some railway stations and bus terminals have IATA codes so that a train or coach journey can be sold on the same ticket as a flight. To a booking system, they behave like airports. You'll rarely see them unless you book a combined air and rail trip.

City codes and why they exist

IATA also assigns codes to cities with more than one airport, such as LON for London and NYC for New York. These aren't places a plane can land. They exist so that one search can cover every airport in the city. Our guide to city codes lists the main ones.

The four-letter codes

A separate body, ICAO, gives airports four-letter codes for pilots and air traffic control. Those follow a regional system and are far more predictable. See IATA and ICAO codes explained.

What this means for learning them

Because codes were handed out over many decades, there's no single rule that explains them all. The best approach is to learn the patterns, such as old names, Canada's Y and the US letter rules, and then learn the exceptions one by one. Our Daily Departure puzzle gives you five a day.
