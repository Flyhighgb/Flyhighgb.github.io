Codes that come from the local name: MUC, WAW and PRG

Munich is MUC because of München, Warsaw is WAW because of Warszawa. When the code follows the local spelling, not the English one.

By Amateurgames1 · Published 16 November 2026

Most English speakers learn city names in English. But many airport codes were chosen by people who used the local name, so the letters only make sense once you know it. Here are the most common ones in Europe, and a few that went the other way.

Codes from the local name

  • MUC: Munich, from München. The U and C are both there.
  • WAW: Warsaw, from Warszawa, the Polish name.
  • PRG: Prague, from Praha, the Czech name.
  • BEG: Belgrade, from Beograd, the Serbian name.

Codes from the English name

Plenty of codes follow the English spelling instead, which can be just as confusing if you know the city by its local name:

  • VIE: Vienna, not Wien.
  • CPH: Copenhagen, not København: CoPenHagen.
  • CGN: Cologne, not Köln.
  • FLR: Florence, not Firenze.
  • VCE: Venice, not Venezia.
  • ATH: Athens, not Athína.
  • LIS: Lisbon, which works for Lisboa too.

Codes from a nearby place

Some codes follow the town or district where the airport sits rather than the city it serves:

  • FCO: Rome, from Fiumicino, the town on the coast.
  • MXP: Milan, from Malpensa.
  • ARN: Stockholm, from Arlanda.
  • KEF: Reykjavík, from Keflavík.
  • ESB: Ankara, from Esenboğa.
  • GRU: São Paulo, from Guarulhos.
  • EZE: Buenos Aires, from Ezeiza.
  • CTS: Sapporo, from Chitose.

Codes from an older name

Others come from a name the city no longer uses, such as BOM for Bombay and PEK for Peking. We've collected those in airport codes that remember an old name.

A quick way to crack them

  1. Try the English name first.
  2. If that fails, try the local spelling. Central and Eastern European codes often follow it.
  3. Then try the town where the airport actually is.
  4. Still nothing? It may be an old name, or a code that had to dodge a clash.
