Airline codes vs airport codes: BA, BAW and LHR

Your flight is BA117 from LHR. Air traffic control calls it Speedbird. Here's how airline codes, airport codes and call signs fit together.

By Amateurgames1 · Published 16 November 2026

A boarding pass mixes two kinds of code that look alike. The three letters for where you're going are an airport code. The two characters at the front of your flight number are an airline code. And behind the scenes, there's a third code and a spoken call sign as well.

Two-character airline codes

IATA gives each airline a two-character designator, used in flight numbers, tickets and timetables. Some are obvious, others less so:

  • BA: British Airways
  • AA: American Airlines
  • DL: Delta
  • UA: United
  • LH: Lufthansa
  • AF: Air France
  • KL: KLM
  • EK: Emirates
  • QF: Qantas
  • SQ: Singapore Airlines
  • FR: Ryanair
  • U2: easyJet

A flight number is the airline code plus a number: BA117 is a British Airways flight, and the number identifies the route and time. The same number usually runs on the same route every day.

Three-letter airline codes

ICAO gives airlines a three-letter code too, used in flight plans and by air traffic control. It's the code you'll see on flight-tracking apps:

  • BAW: British Airways
  • AAL: American Airlines
  • DAL: Delta
  • UAL: United
  • DLH: Lufthansa
  • AFR: Air France
  • UAE: Emirates
  • QFA: Qantas
  • SIA: Singapore Airlines
  • RYR: Ryanair
  • EZY: easyJet

Where they clash with airport codes

Because airline and airport codes are separate lists, the same letters can mean different things. DAL is Delta's airline code, and it's also the airport code for Dallas Love Field (DAL). UAE is Emirates' airline code, but it's also short for the United Arab Emirates, whose airports are DXB, AUH and others. Context tells you which one you're looking at: on a bag tag or departures board, it's an airport.

Call signs

On the radio, controllers don't say "BAW117". Each airline has a spoken call sign. British Airways is "Speedbird", Aer Lingus is "Shamrock", easyJet is "Easy" and Ryanair is simply "Ryanair". So BA117 becomes "Speedbird one one seven".

Putting it together

Take a flight from London Heathrow to New York JFK on British Airways:

  • On your boarding pass: BA117, LHR to JFK.
  • On a flight tracker: BAW117, EGLL to KJFK.
  • On the radio: "Speedbird one one seven".
